The Judge Changed Its Mind

2025-07-22 · dispatch

The nightly eval run came back on Wednesday morning with good news for everyone. The new summarization prompt, v19, scored 7.8 out of 10 on the LLM-as-judge rubric. The production prompt, v18, had scored 7.1 the night before. A 0.7-point jump on a rubric that usually moved by tenths. The prompt engineer who wrote v19 merged it before lunch.

Two weeks later, the customer success team forwarded a cluster of complaints. Account summaries had gotten vaguer. They mentioned fewer numbers, hedged more, and skipped the renewal risk section that account managers actually read.

The engineer who picked it up started with the obvious question: if v19 was so much better, why were people complaining? She re-ran the eval. v19 still scored 7.8. Then she looked at the one number nobody had looked at on Wednesday: v18's score on the same night. It wasn't in the report, because v18 wasn't being evaluated anymore. She dug it out of the run logs. On Wednesday night, v18 had scored 7.7.

The old prompt had improved by 0.6 points overnight without changing a single character.

⚠ The judge alias moved to a new model overnight. Every score rose, including the control that hadn't changed. On the old judge, prompt v19 was 2 points worse than v18.

The eval harness called the judge through the provider's floating alias. On Tuesday night, the provider had moved that alias to a newer model. The new judge was more generous across the board, and it had different tastes: it rewarded fluency and hedged, balanced phrasing, and it penalized the blunt, number-heavy style the account managers liked.

v19 had been written to be smoother. The new judge loved it. Re-scored on the old judge, pinned to its dated snapshot, v19 came in at 6.9, nearly a point below v18.

“The old prompt had improved by 0.6 points overnight without changing a single character.”

Nobody had been watching the judge because the judge wasn't the thing being tested. It was the ruler. And nobody checks whether the ruler has changed length overnight.

The prompt engineer had done what the process asked: write a better prompt, run the eval, ship the winner. The process had no step for asking whether the eval itself had moved.

They reverted to v18 the same day. The process changes took longer and mattered more. The judge is now pinned to a dated snapshot and upgraded on purpose, with a calibration run first. Every eval report includes a frozen control: the current production prompt, scored on the same run, so any drift in the judge shows up as drift in a thing that didn't change. And once a month, two account managers hand-score fifty summaries, and the team tracks the judge's agreement with them. That number had quietly fallen from 0.71 to 0.58 with the new judge, which would have said everything if anyone had been measuring it.

If the baseline's score moves and the baseline didn't change, you're not measuring your model. You're measuring the judge.
