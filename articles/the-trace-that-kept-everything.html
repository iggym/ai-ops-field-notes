The Trace That Kept Everything

2026-08-04 · dispatch

The finding came from a privacy auditor, not an alert. She was walking the data map for the benefits assistant, the chatbot that helped employees with insurance claims, and asked a simple question about the tracing vendor listed under “observability”: what fields do we send them?

Nobody in the room knew. The engineering lead said “latency and token counts, mostly.” The platform engineer opened the vendor's UI to check. The first span he clicked on contained a full Social Security number.

The team had done the work on logging. A redaction filter on the application logger matched SSNs, dates of birth, policy numbers and bank details, and replaced them before anything was written. It had tests. It had been reviewed by security. The logs were clean, and the team had reasonably concluded that the system was clean.

The tracing SDK didn't go through the logger.

That sentence took the team about twenty minutes to understand and most of the next quarter to clean up.

⚠ The redaction filter ran on the logging handler. The tracing SDK captured llm.input from the client call, upstream of it, and shipped it to a vendor with 400-day retention.

It had been added seven months earlier, when the team wanted per-step latency for their retrieval pipeline. The SDK's auto-instrumentation wrapped the LLM client and captured the full prompt and completion as span attributes. That is its default, and it is very useful for debugging. It captured them at the client call, upstream of the logging handler, so the redaction filter never saw them. Every prompt a user typed, including the ones where they pasted a claim form, went to the vendor verbatim.

“The logs were clean, and the team had reasonably concluded that the system was clean.”

The vendor stored spans for 400 days, in a region the company's data processing agreement didn't cover. Rough count: 2.3 million spans with user content, an unknown fraction carrying identifiers. No breach in the attacker sense, but the company now had regulated health and identity data sitting in a system it had never assessed, under a retention policy it had never agreed to.

The cleanup took six weeks: a deletion request to the vendor, a written attestation back, legal review of notification obligations, and a re-run of the data protection impact assessment with tracing in scope this time.

The engineering fix was smaller and more pointed. Content capture is now off by default in the tracing config, enforced by a CI check that fails the build if it's enabled. Where engineers need prompt content to debug, it passes through the same redaction function as the logs, at the SDK's span processor, before export. The data map now lists every exporter in the process, not every vendor in the contract.

Observability tools are built to capture everything because that's what makes them useful. In an LLM system, everything includes whatever the user typed.

You redacted the logs. The traces were a second copy of every prompt, and nobody had put them on the data map.
