The Cache That Answered Last Quarter

2026-09-16 · dispatch

The complaint that started it was polite and specific. A customer had been told by the store's assistant, twice, in writing, that she could return an opened blender within 60 days. The store refused the return under the new policy: 30 days, unopened items only. She attached screenshots. Customer service checked the assistant's logs and saw the same answer she did.

The returns policy had changed seventeen days earlier. Legal had signed off on new wording, the knowledge base was updated the same afternoon, and the retrieval index re-embedded the changed documents overnight. The team had tested it: ask the assistant about returns, get the new policy. It passed.

It passed because the test phrasing was new. Real customers ask the same handful of questions in the same handful of ways.

Six months earlier, the team had put a semantic cache in front of the whole RAG pipeline. Embed the incoming question, look for a cached question with cosine similarity above 0.92, and if one exists, return its stored answer without retrieval or generation. It cut model costs by more than half and dropped median latency to under 50 milliseconds. It was the most popular optimization the team had ever shipped.

The cache key was the meaning of the question. It had nothing to do with the state of the knowledge the answer was built from. When the policy changed, every cached answer about returns stayed valid by the only test the cache applied. “Can I return an opened item?” is exactly as similar to “can i return something i opened” on day 18 as it was on day 1. The cache had a 30-day TTL, and the hot entries were being refreshed by hits faster than they expired.

“The cache key was the meaning of the question. It had nothing to do with the state of the knowledge the answer was built from.”

A replay of seventeen days of traffic found roughly 4,100 conversations where the assistant had served the old returns policy from cache. The company honored all of them, which was the right call and an expensive one.

The cache survived, with changes. Each entry now stores the IDs and versions of the documents that were retrieved to produce it. When a document changes, every cache entry that depended on it is evicted in the same job that re-embeds it. Policy-class documents, such as returns, pricing and warranties, are excluded from caching entirely. And the eval suite now runs every test question twice, once cold and once warm, and fails if the answers differ.

Caching a deterministic function is easy. Caching a probabilistic system whose truth lives in a separate database means you are caching a join, and you need to invalidate on both sides.

A semantic cache doesn't know what changed. It only knows what's similar, and last quarter's answer is very similar.
