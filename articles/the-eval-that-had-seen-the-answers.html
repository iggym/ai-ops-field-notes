The Eval That Had Seen the Answers

2025-06-24 · dispatch

The fine-tuned model shipped on a Thursday with the best eval numbers the team had ever posted. Ninety-four percent of support tickets resolved correctly on the offline set, against 81% for the base model it replaced. The launch note had a chart. People reacted with the rocket emoji.

Three weeks later, the support operations lead asked why the assistant's real-world resolution rate had gone down. Not up by thirteen points. Down by four.

The ML engineer on rotation assumed a production problem first: a prompt mismatch between eval and serving, a retrieval regression, a traffic shift. She checked each one. The serving prompt matched. Retrieval was unchanged. The ticket mix was normal for the season.

Then she went back to where the eval set came from.

Both the training data and the eval set had been drawn from the same export: eighteen months of resolved tickets. The pipeline split them 90/10 and removed exact duplicates across the split. That sounded careful. But support tickets are mostly the same few hundred problems asked in slightly different words, and many of the answers come from macros. Two tickets that say “I never got my reset email” and “password reset email not arriving” are not exact duplicates. To a model that trained on one of them, they are the same question.

⚠ Exact-match dedup missed paraphrases and templated tickets. 38% of eval items had a near-duplicate in the training set. On clean data, the fine-tune was slightly worse.

She ran a near-duplicate check using embeddings with a similarity threshold of 0.9. Four hundred fifty-six of the 1,200 eval tickets had a close match in the training set, often with an identical macro response. The fine-tuned model had not learned to solve those tickets. It had learned the answers.

“The fine-tuned model had not learned to solve those tickets. It had learned the answers.”

She then built a clean eval from tickets created after the training export's cutoff date, so overlap was impossible. On that set, the fine-tune scored 79%. The base model scored 81%. The fine-tune was slightly worse at the job and much better at the test, and the team had shipped it on the strength of the test.

The rollback took ten minutes. Resolution rates recovered within a week.

Nobody had been careless. The split was random, the dedup was real, and the score was honestly computed. It just answered a different question from the one the team was asking.

The process changes were more durable. Eval sets are now split by time, never randomly: train on everything before a date, evaluate on everything after it. Near-duplicate detection runs across the boundary as a CI check, and the build fails above 2% overlap. Every eval report carries a contamination estimate next to the headline score. And any fine-tune that beats its base by more than five points gets a second look before anyone posts a chart, because a jump that big on a mature task is more often a leak than a breakthrough.

An eval that overlaps your training data isn't measuring the model. It's measuring its memory.
