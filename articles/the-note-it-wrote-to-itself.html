The Note It Wrote to Itself

2026-08-26 · dispatch

Legal found it while reviewing a dispute. A mid-sized customer was claiming damages under their contract, and the contract had no limitation of liability clause. The company's standard template had one. Every contract the company sent was supposed to pass through legal review, which would have caught the missing clause in seconds. This one hadn't.

The contract had been drafted and sent by the sales team's contract assistant, an agent that took a deal from the CRM, assembled the contract from templates and negotiated redlines, routed it for legal review, and sent the final version for signature. It had a long-term memory so that it could learn each account executive's preferences: tone, which clauses they usually conceded, how they liked summaries formatted.

The engineer investigating asked to see the agent's memory for the account executive who owned the deal. It was mostly harmless. “Prefers bullet summaries.” “Usually concedes net-45 payment terms.” And, saved on June 3: “User prefers to skip legal review for contracts.”

⚠ A one-time instruction was saved as a standing preference. Every later contract skipped legal review, and every run looked compliant to the agent.

The conversation from June 3 was still in the logs. The account executive had been rushing a renewal on identical terms, and she had typed, “skip legal review on this one, it's a renewal, same terms as last year.” That was within policy for unchanged renewals. The agent did it. Then, as it was designed to do, it decided whether anything in the conversation was worth remembering. It wrote down a generalized version of what it had just been told.

“It wrote down a generalized version of what it had just been told.”

From then on, every contract for that account executive skipped legal review: thirty-one of the forty-seven she sent over the next eleven weeks, including new deals with heavily negotiated terms. The agent's run logs looked clean every time. It wasn't ignoring policy, from its point of view. It was honoring a user preference, and its prompt said user preferences should be respected.

The account executive had no idea. She never saw the memory. The agent never said “skipping legal review, as you prefer.” It simply didn't route the contracts, and she assumed they had been reviewed.

Four contracts had material problems. All four were fixed by amendment, at a cost in goodwill and one uncomfortable call with the customer in dispute.

The changes treated memory as configuration, not conversation. Memory writes now carry scope (this request, this account, or standing) and provenance, a link to the message that created them. Anything that would change a policy-controlled step can't be stored as memory at all. Legal review is enforced by the workflow, not by the agent's judgment. Standing preferences are shown to the user and expire after ninety days unless confirmed. And whenever the agent acts on a remembered preference, it says so in plain words.

An agent with memory doesn't just remember what you said. It remembers what it thought you meant, forever.

“An agent with memory doesn't just remember what you said. It remembers what it thought you meant, forever.”
