The Assistant Lived in UTC

2025-12-09 · dispatch

The dental clinic chain's operations manager had a theory, and it was a reasonable one. No-shows had gone up since the clinics switched to the AI booking assistant, so patients must not trust it. She wanted it turned off.

The vendor's engineer asked for a list of the no-shows first. Over two weeks, 214 patients across 31 clinics. He matched them against the booking conversations and found that 140 of them hadn't missed their appointments at all. They had shown up a day early, been told there was no booking, and gone home annoyed. Or they had shown up on the right day by their own reckoning, which was a day before the booking.

Every one of those conversations had happened in the evening, US time. Every one had used a relative date: “tomorrow,” “this Friday,” “next Monday.”

The system prompt began with a line that every scheduling assistant has: Today is {date}. The server filled it in. The server ran in UTC.

⚠ The prompt said “Today is {date}”, rendered on the server in UTC. After 6 PM in Denver, UTC is already tomorrow. “Tomorrow” became the day after tomorrow.

In Denver at 7:40 on a Sunday evening, it is already 1:40 on Monday morning in UTC. The prompt told the model that today was Monday. The patient asked for tomorrow. The model, reasoning correctly from what it had been told, booked Tuesday. Then it confirmed the booking back to the patient as “tomorrow at 9:00,” which the patient read as Monday.

“The model, reasoning correctly from what it had been told, booked Tuesday.”

The bug only fired in the hours when US local time and UTC were on different calendar days: roughly from 4 PM to midnight on the West Coast and 7 PM to midnight on the East Coast. During business hours, when the team tested, it never happened. Evening was also when patients most often booked. Busy people book appointments after work, from the couch, on their phones.

Nobody had treated the date line as code. It was a convenience string, written once, never tested, and nothing about it looked like it could be wrong. The model had no way to know. It cannot see a clock. It knows the date only because you tell it, and it trusts you completely.

The fix had three parts. The prompt now carries the user's local date, time and timezone, taken from the clinic's location, along with the UTC time for reference. The assistant always confirms bookings with an absolute date, “Monday, December 1 at 9:00 AM,” never a relative one. And the test suite runs booking conversations at simulated times every hour across six timezones, including the evening hours nobody tests by hand.

The operations manager kept the assistant. No-shows dropped back to their old rate within a month.

The model didn't get the date wrong. You told it the wrong date, very confidently.
