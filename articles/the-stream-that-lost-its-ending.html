The Stream That Lost Its Ending

2025-11-11 · dispatch

The complaint came from a traveler who had been refused boarding in Lagos. The airline's agent had asked for an airport transit visa for his connection in Frankfurt. He didn't have one, because the travel booking assistant had told him he didn't need a Schengen visa for a layover. He attached the screenshot. The answer on his phone was confident, well-formatted, and ended mid-paragraph.

The support team pulled the conversation from the server logs. The server's version of the answer was two sentences longer. The last sentence said that holders of his passport needed an airport transit visa even without leaving the airport.

Same conversation, two different answers. The server had sent the full one. The phone had shown the short one.

The assistant streamed its responses over server-sent events, token by token, so users saw text appear immediately. The model finished generating, the server logged a 200 with the full completion, and the request was marked successful. The engineer on call traced the path between the server and the phone and found the gap: the company's CDN had an idle timeout of 30 seconds on streaming connections. On slow mobile networks, with long answers and pauses while the model looked things up, some streams went quiet for long enough that the CDN closed them.

⚠ The CDN closed idle streams after 30 seconds. On slow mobile connections, long answers were cut mid-stream. The client rendered whatever it had as a finished answer.

When the stream closed early, the mobile client did exactly what it had been written to do: stop the typing animation and show the text it had. It never checked for the [DONE] event that marked a complete answer. A stream cut by a timeout and a stream that ended normally looked identical to it.

“A stream cut by a timeout and a stream that ended normally looked identical to it.”

A week of CDN logs showed 2.7% of mobile streams closing without the final event. Most cuts landed in harmless places, a missing closing pleasantry or a dropped “happy travels.” Some didn't. Travel advice is often structured as a general rule followed by an exception, and the exception comes last.

Nobody had built a metric for this, because the server never saw it. Every log line the team owned said the answer had been delivered.

The fixes were small and spread across three teams. The CDN timeout for streaming routes went up to five minutes, with a heartbeat comment sent every ten seconds to keep slow streams alive. The client now treats a stream without [DONE] as an error: it shows what arrived, marks it clearly as incomplete, and offers to retry. And the client reports completion back to the server, so “answer fully delivered” is a measured rate on the dashboard instead of an assumption.

The traveler was rebooked and reimbursed. The airline asked for a copy of the incident review.
