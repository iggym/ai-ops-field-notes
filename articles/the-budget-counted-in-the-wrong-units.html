The Budget Counted in the Wrong Units

2025-08-19 · dispatch

The escalation came through the account team, not the pager. The company's largest customer in Japan, a manufacturer with eleven thousand employees on the internal help assistant, said the bot had become useless. One in three questions got the same reply: “I couldn't find information about that.” The answers existed. They were in the manufacturer's own knowledge base, which the assistant searched.

The platform dashboard said the assistant was fine. Error rate 0.4%, well inside the SLO. Retrieval hit rate normal. Latency normal. By every number the team reviewed each week, the assistant was healthy.

The engineer on call filtered the logs to the Japanese tenant. The error rate there was 31%. Almost all of it was one error: the provider rejecting the request with 400: context length exceeded. The assistant's fallback path caught the 400 and returned the polite “couldn't find” message, which the dashboard counted as a handled response.

Context overflow shouldn't have been possible. The orchestration layer had a budget step that packed retrieved passages into the prompt until it hit 90% of the context window, then stopped. The engineer read the budget function. It estimated tokens as characters divided by four.

⚠ The budget used characters ÷ 4. That's roughly right for English and badly wrong for Japanese. Overflowing requests got a 400, and the fallback said “I couldn't find that.”

Characters divided by four is a well-known rule of thumb for English. It is not a law of nature. For Japanese text, where a single character often maps to one or more tokens, the real count was closer to three times the estimate. The budget step thought it was packing 90% of the window. For Japanese passages it was packing about 280%.

“Characters divided by four is a well-known rule of thumb for English. It is not a law of nature.”

The Japanese tenant was the first customer with a large non-Latin knowledge base. Its traffic was about 1.3% of the total, so even a 31% failure rate inside it barely moved the global error rate. The SLO was computed across all tenants. By that definition, nothing was wrong.

The engineer who wrote the estimator had known it was approximate. The comment above it said # good enough for now. For two years, it was. Every customer in those two years had written mostly in English.

The fix replaced the estimate with the model's actual tokenizer, run locally, with a safety margin of 5%. The budget step now fails loudly in staging if its count ever differs from the provider's reported usage by more than 10%. Error rates are segmented by tenant and by language on the main dashboard, and the SLO applies to each tenant, not only to the total. And the fallback no longer pretends a 400 is a search miss. It says that something went wrong and logs it as an error, which it is.

The customer's account manager spent a month rebuilding trust. The engineer spent an afternoon on the fix.
