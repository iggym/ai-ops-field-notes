The Failover That Crossed the Ocean

2026-04-07 · dispatch

The email came from a customer's data protection officer at 9:12 on a Wednesday morning. She had noticed a response header in her team's API logs, x-served-region: us-east, on requests from the previous afternoon. Her contract said the company's AI features processed EU personal data in the EU. She asked, politely, for an explanation by Friday. The platform team didn't have one ready.

The platform team checked the incident log for Tuesday. There was no incident. Availability had been 100%. The only blip on the dashboards was a p95 latency bump of about 180 milliseconds that lasted most of the afternoon and resolved itself.

The latency bump was the incident.

The company ran its LLM traffic through an internal gateway that routed each request to a model endpoint. For EU tenants, the primary was the provider's EU region. When that endpoint started returning elevated errors at 13:00, the gateway's health check marked it degraded and did what it was built to do: fail over to the next healthy endpoint on the list.

⚠ The EU endpoint degraded. The gateway's failover list was ordered by latency, not jurisdiction, and the next healthy region was in the US.

The failover list had been written two years earlier, when the gateway served only US customers. It was ordered by latency. When the EU tenants were added, someone put the EU endpoint at the top of the list for those tenants and left the rest of the list alone. Second on the list was us-east. For five hours and ten minutes, about 412,000 requests from EU tenants, carrying support tickets, contract text and employee names, were processed in the United States.

“The failover list had been written two years earlier, when the gateway served only US customers.”

Nothing broke. That was the problem. The gateway's job was availability, and by that measure the afternoon had been a success. Data residency wasn't a constraint the router knew about. It existed in contracts, in a compliance spreadsheet and in the sales team's answers to security questionnaires. It didn't exist in the routing config. The router had done its job perfectly. Nobody had told it the job included staying in Europe.

The legal review took three weeks and involved notifications to eleven customers. The engineering fix took two days. Residency is now a hard constraint on every route: EU tenant traffic can only be served by EU endpoints, and if none is healthy, the gateway degrades gracefully, queuing non-urgent requests and returning a clear “temporarily unavailable” for interactive ones, instead of failing over across a border. The serving region is a dimension on every dashboard. And a nightly check samples gateway logs for any request whose serving region doesn't match its tenant's residency policy. It has found nothing since. It is the alert the team most wants to stay quiet.

Availability and compliance are both requirements. Your failover only knew about one of them.
