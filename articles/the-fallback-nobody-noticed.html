The Fallback Nobody Noticed

2026-09-02 · dispatch

The support director raised it in a weekly business review, not an incident channel. Escalations from the AI assistant to human agents were up 38% over two weeks. Her team was drowning. Was the assistant broken?

Engineering pulled up the dashboards and said no. Success rate 99.97%. Error rate flat. And p95 latency had actually improved, from 2.6 seconds to 1.8. If anything, the assistant looked healthier than it had all quarter.

That latency improvement should have been the clue.

Nobody on the support side could see the router, and nobody on the platform side could see escalations. Each team's dashboard was green by its own definition.

The model router had a fallback chain: primary model first, and on a timeout or a 429 rate-limit response, retry once on a smaller, cheaper, faster model. It had been added after a provider outage the previous spring, and it worked as designed. When the primary was unavailable, users still got an answer.

Nine days before the director's meeting, a marketing campaign had pushed traffic up 60%. The team had not raised their rate limit tier with the provider. The primary started returning 429s at peak, and the router did exactly what it was built to do.

⚠ 41% of requests had been served by the fallback model for nine days. The router counted a fallback as a success.

The router logged a fallback as a success, because from the transport's point of view it was one. Nothing on the main dashboard broke out traffic by serving model. The faster p95 was the smaller model pulling the average down. For nine days, 41% of conversations, concentrated in the busiest hours, were handled by a model that had never been evaluated on the support domain. It was fluent. It was polite. It was much worse at multi-step account problems, and it gave up and escalated far more readily.

“The router logged a fallback as a success, because from the transport's point of view it was one.”

The fix for the immediate problem took one email: raise the rate limit tier. Fallback traffic dropped below 2% within the hour, and escalations returned to baseline over the next three days.

They also replayed 500 fallback conversations through the primary model. The primary resolved 71% of them without a human. The fallback had managed 44%.

The structural fixes took longer. The fallback rate became a top-line SLO with its own alert, at 5% over 15 minutes. Every response now carries the serving model as a trace attribute, and the quality dashboard is split by it. The fallback model went through the same offline eval suite as the primary, and for two intents where it scored badly, the router now returns a graceful “please hold for an agent” instead of falling back at all.

And the team rewrote one line in their runbook. A fallback is a degraded mode, not a success state, and degraded modes need a clock on them.

A fallback that silently changes quality isn't resilience. It's an outage you've chosen not to measure.
